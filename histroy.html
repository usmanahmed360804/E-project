FROM THE BEGINNING

A Short Story of Climbing

People have crossed mountains for trade, travel and survival for thousands of years, but climbing for its own sake is much newer. Mountaineering as a sport grew out of curiosity about nature, scientific study of the Alps and a sense of challenge.

Over about 240 years it has moved from a few daring ascents of Alpine peaks to organized clubs, new techniques, the race for the Himalayan giants, and today's mix of expedition climbing and lightweight alpine style.

Early Ascents

Before 1786: scattered climbs for religion, war and curiosity.

Golden Age

1854 to 1865: most major Alpine summits are climbed.

Himalayan Era

1950 to 1964: all fourteen 8,000-metre peaks are climbed.

FROM THE BEGINNING

History of Mountaineering

1336

Petrarch on Mont Ventoux

The Italian poet Petrarch is traditionally said to have climbed Mont Ventoux in France just to enjoy the view, an early example of climbing for pleasure.

1492

Mont Aiguille

Antoine de Ville and his men climbed the cliff-ringed Mont Aiguille in France using ladders and ropes, on the order of King Charles VIII.

1760

A Prize for Mont Blanc

The Swiss scientist Horace-Bénédict de Saussure offered a reward to the first person to find a route to the summit of Mont Blanc, which sparked interest in the peak.

1786

First Major Alpine Ascent

Jacques Balmat and Michel-Gabriel Paccard reached the summit of Mont Blanc. De Saussure followed the next year with a scientific expedition.

1808

First Woman on Mont Blanc

Marie Paradis, a local servant, was helped to the top of Mont Blanc and became the first woman known to reach it.

1854

Golden Age of Alpinism

Many important Alpine peaks were climbed and modern climbing techniques developed. The Wetterhorn ascent by Alfred Wills is often used to mark the start of this period.

1857

The Alpine Club

The Alpine Club was founded in London, the first mountaineering club in the world. Swiss, Italian and French clubs followed in the 1860s and 1870s.

1865

The Matterhorn

Edward Whymper's team made the first ascent of the Matterhorn. Four of the seven climbers fell to their deaths on the way down, and the tragedy led to debate about the risks of climbing.

1895

First Attempt on an 8,000-Metre Peak

British climber Albert Mummery tried to climb Nanga Parbat in the Himalaya and disappeared with two Gurkha companions, starting the long story of high-altitude climbing.

1924

Mallory and Irvine

George Mallory and Andrew Irvine were last seen high on Everest. Whether they reached the summit is still debated, and Mallory's body was found in 1999.

1938

Eiger North Face

Anderl Heckmair, Ludwig Vörg, Fritz Kasparek and Heinrich Harrer made the first ascent of the Eiger's feared north face in the Swiss Alps.

1950

Annapurna I

A French expedition led by Maurice Herzog became the first to climb an 8,000-metre peak.

1953

Mount Everest

Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay successfully reached the summit of Mount Everest. Later that year, Hermann Buhl climbed Nanga Parbat alone for the final stretch.

1954

K2

An Italian expedition, with Achille Compagnoni and Lino Lacedelli reaching the top, made the first ascent of K2 in the Karakoram.

1964

All Fourteen Climbed

A Chinese expedition climbed Shishapangma, the last of the fourteen 8,000-metre peaks to receive a first ascent.

1975

Junko Tabei

Junko Tabei of Japan became the first woman to reach the summit of Mount Everest.

1978

Everest Without Oxygen

Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler climbed Everest without bottled oxygen. In 1980 Messner repeated the feat alone.

1986

All Fourteen Summits

Reinhold Messner became the first person to climb all fourteen 8,000-metre peaks. Jerzy Kukuczka of Poland completed the list the following year.

1996

Everest Disaster

A storm on May 10 and 11 killed eight climbers on Everest. The event drew worldwide attention to commercial expeditions and crowding on the mountain.

2016

Winter Nanga Parbat

Simone Moro, Alex Txikon and Muhammad Ali Sadpara made the first winter ascent of Nanga Parbat in Pakistan.

2019

Fourteen Peaks in Six Months

Nepali climber Nirmal Purja climbed all fourteen 8,000-metre peaks in six months and six days, a huge drop from the previous record of almost eight years.

2021

First Winter Ascent of K2

A team of ten Nepali climbers reached the summit of K2 in January, the last 8,000-metre peak to be climbed in winter.

Today

Modern Mountaineering

Climbers now use advanced equipment, satellite navigation and modern weather forecasting.

PEOPLE WHO CHANGED CLIMBING

Famous Pioneers

Tenzing Norgay

Sherpa climber who reached the summit of Everest with Edmund Hillary in 1953.

Hermann Buhl

Austrian climber who made a long solo push to the summit of Nanga Parbat in 1953.

Junko Tabei

First woman on Everest in 1975, and later the first woman to climb the Seven Summits.

Reinhold Messner

Italian climber who pushed style and ethics with oxygen-free and solo ascents.

Edward Whymper

English illustrator and climber who led the first ascent of the Matterhorn.

Nirmal Purja

Nepali climber who raised public attention with his fast fourteen-peak project.

HOW CLIMBING EVOLVED

Equipment and Style Through Time

1800s

Wool, Hemp Rope and Hobnailed Boots

Early climbers wore heavy wool clothing and relied on long wooden ice axes, natural fiber ropes and local guides.

1900s

Crampons and Pitons

Metal crampons, pitons and carabiners allowed climbers to tackle steeper ice and rock faces.

1950s

Oxygen and Nylon

Bottled oxygen, nylon ropes and insulated boots made the highest peaks possible to attempt.

1970s

Light and Fast

Climbers began using small teams, lighter gear and less fixed equipment, known as alpine style.

2000s

Technology and Forecasts

GPS, satellite phones, avalanche beacons, synthetic insulation and detailed weather forecasts now support planning and rescue.

THE PEOPLE OF THE HIGH HIMALAYA

The Sherpa Legacy

Sherpa people of Nepal have been central to Himalayan climbing since the early expeditions. They carry loads, fix ropes, set up camps and often make the summit possible for others. Tenzing Norgay's success in 1953 brought wider recognition to this role.

Today many of the most experienced high-altitude climbers are Sherpa, and many expedition teams depend on their skill. The work is also dangerous, and Sherpa guides account for a large share of deaths on Everest.
