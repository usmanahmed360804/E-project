BEFORE YOU GO

Why Mountains Are Risky

Mountains combine steep terrain, extreme weather, thin air and remote locations. Help can be hours or days away, so small problems can quickly become serious ones.

Risks can be grouped into two types. Objective hazards, such as avalanches and rockfall, come from the mountain itself. Subjective hazards, such as poor planning, fatigue and bad decisions, come from the people climbing. Skill and judgment reduce both, but never remove them completely.

KNOW THE RISKS

Mountain Hazards

Avalanches

Rapid movement of snow and ice down a mountain slope.

Bad Weather

Storms, snowfall, strong winds and low visibility.

Hypothermia

Dangerous loss of body temperature in cold conditions.

Rockfall

Loose rocks can fall unexpectedly from steep terrain.

Altitude Sickness

Illness caused by low oxygen when climbing too fast to high elevations.

Frostbite

Freezing of skin and deeper tissue, most often in the fingers, toes, nose and ears.

Crevasses

Deep cracks in glaciers that can be hidden under a thin layer of snow.

Lightning

Exposed ridges and summits are dangerous places during thunderstorms.

Sun and Snow Blindness

Strong ultraviolet light at altitude burns skin and can painfully damage unprotected eyes.

Dehydration

Dry air and heavy effort cause fluid loss, which makes fatigue and altitude illness worse.

Serac and Cornice Collapse

Blocks of glacier ice and overhanging snow can break away with no warning.

Human Factors

Fatigue, overconfidence, group pressure and "summit fever" lead to poor decisions.

THIN AIR

Altitude Illness

Altitude illness can affect anyone, including very fit climbers. It develops when the body does not have time to adapt to lower oxygen levels. The most important treatments are to stop climbing higher and, if symptoms are getting worse, to descend.

Acute Mountain Sickness

The mild and most common form. Symptoms include headache, nausea, dizziness, poor sleep and loss of appetite.

HAPE

High-altitude pulmonary edema, fluid on the lungs. Signs include breathlessness at rest, a persistent cough and extreme tiredness. It is life threatening.

HACE

High-altitude cerebral edema, swelling of the brain. Signs include confusion, loss of balance and strange behavior. It is life threatening.

Climbers can reduce the risk by ascending gradually, resting, drinking enough fluid and not ignoring symptoms. Anyone with signs of HAPE or HACE needs to descend immediately and get medical help.

SNOW SAFETY

Avalanche Awareness

Most avalanches that catch people are triggered by the victim or their group. They happen most often on slopes between about 30 and 45 degrees, especially after fresh snow, strong wind or rapid warming.

Read the Bulletin

Check the local avalanche forecast before every trip, and choose your route to match the danger level.

Watch for Warning Signs

Recent avalanches, cracking snow and a "whumpf" sound underfoot all signal an unstable snowpack.

Expose One at a Time

Cross suspect slopes one person at a time, and keep the rest of the group in a safe place.

Carry the Rescue Kit

A beacon, probe and shovel, plus the training to use them quickly, are essential in avalanche terrain.

COLD CONDITIONS

Hypothermia and Frostbite

Hypothermia Signs

Shivering, clumsiness, slurred speech and confusion. In severe cases the shivering stops, which is an emergency.

Hypothermia Response

Get out of wind and wet, add dry insulation, provide warm sweet drinks if the person is alert, and call for help.

Frostbite Signs

Numb, pale, waxy or hard skin. Early frostnip makes skin white and cold but still soft.

Frostbite Response

Shelter the person, remove wet or tight clothing and warm the area gently without rubbing it. Seek medical care.

STAY IN CONTROL

How Climbers Reduce Risk

Get Trained

Take courses in navigation, rope work, avalanche safety and first aid.

Plan Thoroughly

Research the route, season, forecast and escape options before you leave.

Set Limits

Agree on a turnaround time and decision points, and stick to them.

Choose Partners Well

Climb with people whose skills and goals match yours, and speak up when something feels wrong.

Carry the Right Gear

Helmet, layers, navigation, first aid, shelter and a way to call for help.

Get Insured

Mountain rescue and medical evacuation can be very costly, so travel and rescue insurance is wise.

LESSONS FROM THE PAST

Major Mountain Disasters

1865

Matterhorn

Four climbers died on the descent after the first ascent when a rope broke, and the accident led to public debate on climbing safety.

1996

Everest Storm

A sudden storm and delays on summit day on May 10 and 11 left eight climbers dead, and raised questions about turnaround times and crowding.

2008

K2

A serac collapse near the Bottleneck on August 1 and 2 damaged ropes and left eleven climbers dead.

2014

Khumbu Icefall

An avalanche on Everest's Khumbu Icefall killed sixteen Nepali guides, and led to a major debate about the risks faced by Sherpa workers.

2015

Nepal Earthquake

An earthquake on April 25 triggered an avalanche that hit Everest Base Camp and killed at least nineteen people.

QUICK ANSWERS

Hazard Questions

What is the most common cause of death in the mountains?

Falls and avalanches are leading causes, and altitude illness and exhaustion add to the risk on very high peaks.

Can I prevent altitude sickness?

You cannot guarantee it, but gradual ascent, rest days and staying hydrated greatly reduce the chance. Never ignore worsening symptoms.

Is a guide worth it?

For beginners, yes. Guides know the terrain, check conditions, make safety decisions and can manage an emergency.

What should I do if I get lost?

Stop, stay calm, check your map or GPS, and avoid wandering further. If you cannot find your way, stay put, keep warm and signal for help.
