START HERE

Skills Before Summits

Mountaineering skills are learned in steps. Climbers practise each technique in safe, controlled places, such as a climbing gym, a small crag or a snow slope with a gentle runout, long before using it on a real mountain.

The information on this page is an overview. These skills should be learned hands-on from a qualified instructor, because a mistake with a knot, anchor or rope system can have serious consequences.

05

Ice Axe Self-Arrest

Stopping a slide on a snow slope by rolling onto the axe and digging the pick into the surface.

06

Crampon Technique

Placing all the points flat on snow or ice, keeping the feet apart so the spikes do not catch clothing.

07

Glacier Travel

Moving as a roped team with spacing between climbers, so a fall into a hidden crevasse can be stopped.

08

Crevasse Rescue

Building an anchor and using rope pulley systems to haul a fallen climber out of a crevasse.

09

Building Anchors

Creating a secure attachment point from rock, ice, snow or removable gear, usually with a backup.

10

Acclimatization

Gaining altitude gradually, often climbing high and sleeping low, so the body can adapt to thinner air.

11

Rest Step and Pacing

Pausing briefly on the straightened back leg with each step to save energy on long, steep slopes.

12

Avalanche Rescue

Searching for a buried partner with a beacon and probe, then digging quickly with a shovel.

TIE IT RIGHT

Essential Knots

Figure-Eight Follow-Through

The standard knot for tying the rope to a harness. It is strong and easy to check by eye.

Clove Hitch

An adjustable knot for attaching the rope to an anchor with a carabiner.

Prusik

A friction hitch that grips the rope when loaded and slides when released, used for rescue and backup.

Double Fisherman's

Joins two rope ends together, or closes a loop of cord for a prusik.

Munter Hitch

A friction knot that can belay or lower a climber if a belay device is dropped.

Alpine Butterfly

A loop in the middle of a rope, used to attach team members on a glacier.

STAY SAFE

Mountain Sheltering

Mountain Huts

Permanent or seasonal shelters providing protection from harsh weather.

Camping Tents

Lightweight tents provide temporary accommodation during expeditions.

Snow Shelters

Snow shelters can provide emergency protection from wind and cold.

Bivouac Sacks

A waterproof, windproof cover for a sleeping bag. It is light enough to carry on fast climbs.

Snow Caves

A hollow dug into a deep snowdrift. The snow insulates from wind, and a small vent hole is needed for air.

Emergency Group Shelters

A large lightweight bag that a whole team can sit inside, sharing body heat while waiting out a storm.

READ THE SKY

Weather Awareness

Weather can change quickly in the mountains. Climbers check the forecast before they leave, then keep watching the sky, wind and temperature throughout the day.

Lenticular Clouds

Smooth, lens-shaped clouds over a summit often point to strong high-altitude winds.

Rising Wind

A sudden increase in wind speed can signal an approaching front.

Falling Pressure

A fast drop on a watch or altimeter usually means worsening weather.

Early Starts

Many climbs begin before dawn to finish before afternoon storms and warming snow.

STAY WARM AND DRY

The Layering System

Base Layer

Next to the skin. It moves sweat away from the body and is usually made of wool or synthetic fabric.

Insulation Layer

Fleece or a down or synthetic jacket traps warm air and is added or removed as the temperature changes.

Shell Layer

A waterproof, windproof outer jacket and trousers protect against rain, snow and wind.

FUEL THE CLIMB

Food, Water and Energy

Drink Regularly

Dry air and hard work dehydrate climbers quickly. Insulated bottles stop water freezing.

Eat Often

Small, frequent snacks such as nuts, bars and dried fruit keep energy steady.

Melt Snow Safely

Where no water is available, climbers melt snow on a stove, starting with a little water in the pot.

Rest and Recover

Sleep and rest days let muscles recover and help the body adjust to altitude.

WHEN THINGS GO WRONG

Emergency Procedures

Even well-prepared teams can face an emergency. Knowing a few basic steps in advance helps climbers stay calm and act quickly.

Stop and Assess

Check for danger to the team, treat injuries and decide whether to descend or wait for help.

Signal for Help

A common distress signal is six whistle blasts or light flashes in a minute, then a minute of silence, repeated.

Call Rescue

Use a phone, radio or satellite communicator to contact rescue services with your exact location.

First Aid

Carry a kit and training to treat cuts, sprains, hypothermia and frostbite until help arrives.

REMEMBER

Golden Rules of the Mountains

The Summit Is Optional

Reaching the top is only half of the climb. Turning back in poor conditions is a strength.

Set a Turnaround Time

Agree on a time to turn around before you start, and stick to it.

Tell Someone

Leave your route and expected return time with a trusted person.

Double-Check

Partners should check each other's knots, harness buckles and anchors before every climb.

QUICK ANSWERS

Technique Questions

Where can I learn these skills?

Join a basic mountaineering course with a certified guide or a mountaineering club. Many climbing gyms also teach rope handling and belaying.

Which skill should I learn first?

Navigation and weather awareness, then rope handling, knots and belaying. Snow and ice skills come after that.

How often should I practise?

Regularly. Rescue skills in particular fade quickly, so many climbers refresh them every season before heading out.

Can I learn from videos alone?

Videos help with understanding, but safety skills need hands-on practice with someone who can check your work.
